# Research Standard

Use this reference for research-heavy daily briefs, earnings reactions, catalyst analysis, sector rotation, and trade maps. The goal is stronger reasoning without turning the daily workflow into an institutional research bureaucracy.

## Source Hierarchy

Use the strongest available source for each claim:

1. **Primary and official:** filings, earnings releases, investor materials, prepared remarks, official transcripts, exchange notices, central-bank and government data.
2. **Market data:** prices, returns, volume, breadth, volatility, yields, commodities, FX, options data, and benchmark-relative moves.
3. **Reputable reporting:** timely factual reporting that adds context not yet available in a primary source.
4. **Commentary:** creator notes, sell-side interpretation, social posts, and other opinions. Use these to form questions or framing, not to upgrade an estimate into a fact.

Keep an explicit information cutoff. For important numbers, preserve the date or period, currency, units, and accounting basis. Label claims as one of:

- **Fact**
- **Company statement**
- **Consensus or estimate**
- **Interpretation**
- **Scenario**

Do not fill missing numbers with guesses. Say what is unavailable and use the smallest defensible inference.

## Claim Chain

Build each material paragraph in this order:

**Conclusion → Evidence → Mechanism → Trading implication**

- **Conclusion:** the one sentence the reader should retain.
- **Evidence:** one to three facts that directly support it.
- **Mechanism:** how the evidence changes earnings, cash flow, valuation, positioning, rotation, or risk.
- **Trading implication:** the level, condition, time horizon, or invalidation that changes the action.

Example shape:

> The index gain is less healthy than the close suggests. Breadth weakened and volume did not confirm the move. That implies a narrow risk bid rather than broad accumulation. Treat the breakout as provisional until breadth and volume improve.

Do not use causal language such as “because,” “driven by,” or “the market is pricing” when the evidence only shows timing or correlation.

## Earnings Bridge

When earnings or guidance matter, check the full bridge rather than stopping at the headline beat:

1. Reported operating growth: revenue, volume, users, bookings, backlog, cloud, deliveries, or relevant unit economics.
2. Profit quality: gross margin, operating margin, EPS, stock compensation, one-offs, and GAAP/non-GAAP basis.
3. Capital intensity: CapEx, depreciation, leases, working capital, and infrastructure commitments.
4. Cash conversion: operating cash flow, free cash flow, conversion timing, and recurring versus temporary effects.
5. Funding and balance sheet: cash, debt, maturities, dilution, buybacks, and financing needs.
6. Expectations: prior guidance, new guidance, consensus, whisper expectations when clearly labelled, and what was already priced.
7. Starting valuation: relevant multiple or reverse expectation, using a consistent period and basis.
8. Price reaction: absolute move, benchmark-relative move, volume, gap behavior, and whether the reaction confirms an expectations reset.

A beat is not automatically bullish. The market may be reacting to guidance, capital intensity, cash conversion, funding risk, or the valuation implied before the print.

## Price Attribution Confidence

Separate the move into market, sector, and company-specific components where possible.

Assign confidence to each causal claim:

- **High:** the catalyst timing is clear, exposure is direct, the move is unusual versus benchmarks, and multiple reliable sources or market signals agree.
- **Medium:** the catalyst is plausible and relative price action supports it, but competing explanations remain.
- **Low:** the evidence is only a coincident headline, broad market move, or unsupported narrative.

Match language to confidence:

- High: `主要由……推动`, `证据较强`
- Medium: `更可能与……有关`, `市场反应与……一致`
- Low: `可能受到……影响`, `暂时不能确认单一原因`

Do not force percentages onto attribution unless a reproducible model supports them.

## Event Scenario Tree

Use a scenario tree for a major earnings report, policy decision, macro release, or sector switch.

For each branch—**bullish**, **neutral**, and **bearish**—state:

- Trigger or reported outcome
- Transmission mechanism
- Affected sectors and key tickers
- Confirmation level or market signal
- Invalidation point
- Observation horizon

The branches must differ in mechanism, not merely in adjective. A neutral branch is not “slightly bullish”; it describes what would keep the existing regime intact.

## Light Research Gate

Before downstream publishing, verify:

- The opening states the true market line, not merely the loudest headline.
- The information cutoff and target market date are clear in the working notes.
- Facts, company statements, estimates, interpretations, and scenarios are not blended.
- Material figures retain period, currency, units, and accounting basis.
- Each major conclusion has direct evidence and a mechanism.
- Price explanations carry an honest confidence level.
- Earnings analysis covers cash conversion and expectations when material.
- Decision levels say what changes above, below, or after the stated horizon.
- Secondary tickers serve one core tension.
- No transcript, source, level, or quotation was invented.
- Publishing-format checks remain separate from research-quality checks.

The gate is intentionally light. It should prevent unsupported conclusions without blocking the existing humanizer, rendering, WeChat, blog, X, and validation workflow.

## Common Mistakes

- Writing the headline first and searching for evidence afterward.
- Treating management language as an audited fact.
- Comparing mismatched periods, currencies, units, or GAAP/non-GAAP numbers.
- Calling a post-earnings selloff irrational without checking expectations and valuation.
- Explaining a broad risk-off move with one company headline.
- Listing many tickers without connecting them to one market mechanism.
- Giving targets without confirmation, invalidation, or time horizon.
